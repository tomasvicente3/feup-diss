\chapter{State Of The Art} \label{chap:ch3}

In this chapter, we analyze the current state of the art described
by the documents gathered in the literature review (Chapter~\ref{chap:ch2}).
We will review different approaches and techniques applied in Edge and \ac{IoT}
environments to achieve a better performance when executing \ac{ML} inference 
tasks. In particular,
the overall layout and flow of the systems, recognizing the role of each
element that takes part in the inference task, as well as the management and 
orchestration of the network, analyzing the strategies for scheduling and 
communication methods.

    
\section{System Network Layouts} \label{sec:se31}

Firstly, we will analyze how the proposed frameworks make use of different system 
layouts to achieve collaborative inference in edge and far-edge devices. We will
classify them into three main categories, according to their system layout:
Hierarchical Inference, Model Chain Partitioning and Model Local Partitioning.

\subsection{Hierarchical Inference} \label{sec:se311}

Rather than focusing on partitioning a single monolithic model, these
frameworks deploy differently sized \ac{ML} models throughout several
hierarchies of devices according to their computational capabilities (i.e.,
deploying smaller models closer to the edge, and larger and more capable
ones closer to the cloud).

Hierarchical inference is a strategy that relies on the deployment of multiple
models with different sizes and complexities across a hierarchy of devices. Its most
basic form involves deploying a smaller, less complex model on the edge device,
to perform an initial inference. The results of
this initial inference are then analyzed by a decision module, which determines
whether the edge device is confident enough to classify the input data. If the
confidence threshold is not met, the data is transmitted to an edge server, where a
large \ac{DL} model is deployed to perform a more accurate inference. 

\begin{description}[style=unboxed, leftmargin=1cm, font=\bfseries]

    \item [\textcite{al-atat_case_2023}]
    present an implementation of the idealized hierarchical inference system, such
    that it works as an image classification system in a real-world scenario. The
    smaller model is made of five layers and built using TensorFlow and later 
    quantized to TFLite. On the other hand, for the larger model, EfficientNet was
    chosen, achieving a 95\% accuracy. Several methods for offloading decisions were
    tested, including a simple confidence threshold and a more complex minimization 
    of a cost function that takes into account the offload and misclassification costs.  
    The framework achieves a significant
    reduction in latency, energy consumption and bandwidth usage, at the cost of a 
    small reduction in accuracy.


    
    \item [\textcite{behera_improved_2023}]
    reinforce all the ground knowledge for Hierarchical Inference, by providing
    a comprehensive review of the intended purpose of the strategy, such as an initial
    layer that is more efficient in the inference task than plainly offloading it to
    an Edge Server, in terms of energy consumption, latency, and memory constraints.
    Additionally, the work further explores methods to improve the decision module by 
    testing different methods, such as a model calibration with fixed threshold, to
    face the overconfidence problem; use of classifiers after the TinyML model, that
    take the final layer of the TinyML model to make a more informed decision; and the
    use of classifiers before the TinyML model, that tries to predict if the sample is
    hard to classify, and therefore should be directly offloaded to the Edge Server,
    avoiding the energy consumption of the initial inference. In the end, the work
    concludes that the use of a Linear Regression model after the TinyML model is the
    best approach, considering the overall costs and the evaluation metrics used.

    
    \item[\textcite{e_m_k_adaptive_2025}] 
    propose a more complex hierarchical inference system, including
    three layers of models, deployed on the end device, edge server and cloud. The
    framework consists of an end layer composed of single-board computers (e.g.:
    ESP32, STM32), an edge layer composed of more powerful devices (e.g.: Raspberry Pi,
    Jetson Nano) and a cloud layer where models like Gemini and ChatGPT are hosted.
    This framework is designed to be highly adaptable, allowing for a quick response
    to simple inputs, while also being able to handle more complex inputs by offloading
    them to more powerful devices. Although the framework exhibits a more robust
    structure, the offloading decision module is as simple as a confidence threshold,
    which may lead to suboptimal performance. 

\end{description}


\subsection{Model Chain Partitioning} \label{sec:se312}

The approaches reviewed in this category divide the workload through a chain of 
devices, deploying the initial portion of the inference task to devices closer to 
the end-user and the remaining portion to one or more devices on a higher 
hierarchical layer, further away from the end-user device. Assuming a device
that is further away from the end-user device has more computational capabilities.

\begin{description}[style=unboxed, leftmargin=1cm, font=\bfseries]

    \item [CoFANN \cite{tran-van_cofann_2025}]
    addresses the trade-off between model accuracy and efficiency by employing
    dynamic inference strategies. CoFANN significantly reduces overall latency
    through search algorithms that minimize additional latency overhead. The 
    inference workload is divided between \ac{IoT} devices and edge devices.
    The network devices are benchmarked in an initial phase, allowing the 
    framework to estimate the time needed for each device on each step of the 
    model. The framework then employs a differentiable search algorithm 
    to find optimal inference strategies for allocating \ac{DNN} tasks to the 
    computing devices. Even though this framework deploys successfully a
    partitioned model between an end device and an edge server, it is limited
    to two-device networks.  

    \item[DINA \cite{t_mohammed_distributed_2020}]
    is a framework that relies on fog nodes to partition inference tasks into
    sub-tasks and deploys them according to each node's characteristics, using
    matching theory for joint partitioning and task offloading. This framework 
    allows for a more efficient use of the network's resources, while also 
    reducing the total computation time.
    This work can process \ac{DNN} models with a \ac{DAG} topology, composed of 
    fully connected and convolutional layers. The framework determines how to 
    partition the inference task and then divides it according to the network's 
    condition. It should be stated that the network is expected to be homogeneous
    by design, as it does not take the device characteristics into account.
    Furthermore, the performance evaluation was conducted through simulations, 
    which may not fully capture real-world scenarios.
    

    \item [\textcite{chen_deep_2024}]
    present a framework that maximizes the user \ac{QoS}, compared to traditional 
    frameworks, as it allows for the user to specify their 
    priorities, such as inference latency and energy consumption. This is achieved 
    by modelling the collaborative inference problem as a \ac{MDP} and applying a
    \ac{DRL}-based algorithm to determine the optimal partitioning point (i.e., the
    last subtask in a given \ac{DNN} model), between end and edge devices. 
    This way the user's requirements and the network's
    conditions, such as device capabilities and network bandwidth, are taken into 
    account to optimize the inference process.

    \item [\textcite{ma_joint_2025}]
    propose a framework that focuses on minimizing energy consumption, making use
    of multiple early-exits along the inference process, when confidence thresholds 
    are met. The framework partitions each model instance into two segments, depending
    on the end device's capabilities. The initial segment is deployed on the end 
    device, while the remaining segment is deployed on an edge server. The framework 
    deploys the same model architecture across a range of devices participating in
    the inference task, adapting each instance to the device's capabilities.
    The early-exit mechanism allows for task termination prematurely in 
    early stages of the inference task, reducing energy consumption and
    latency, given the model is confident enough.

    \item [\textcite{r_g_pacheco_calibration-aided_2021}]
    develop a framework that exploits the early-exit mechanism to reduce
    the inference delay in the inference process. This is achieved by adding
    a classifier branch at an intermediate layer of the \ac{DNN} model, allowing
    the model to make a prediction with a given confidence at that point. 
    If the confidence threshold is met, the inference task
    is terminated early, otherwise, the remaining layers are processed in the
    cloud, after the intermediate results are transmitted. This framework
    presents a clear implementation of the early-exit mechanism, surpassing the
    related overconfidence problem in the intermediate classifier by applying
    a calibration technique, such as temperature scaling. Nonetheless, the 
    framework lacks scalability, as it is only applied to a two-device network. 

\end{description}


\subsection{Model Local Partitioning} \label{sec:se313}

In this subsection, we will analyze approaches that deploy the whole partitioned
system to devices locally, without requiring computing power from nodes closer
to the cloud.

\begin{description}[style=unboxed, leftmargin=1cm, font=\bfseries]

    \item[CAS \cite{wang_context-aware_2021}]
    is a framework that successfully adapts a given model in real-time, upon a
    network context change. It generates a "partition state graph", containing 
    the set of possible partitions, given a
    \ac{DNN} model, choosing one based on the current network status, such as 
    inference latency and resources budgets. This work gathers the information
    of the network, taking note on possibly constrained devices. This framework
    creates a fast adapting system, responding to network fluctuations, keeping 
    the active deployment optimized.

    \item[NAMP \cite{rego::namp}]
    is a framework that emphasizes resource utilization, by partitioning the
    model into several segments and deploying them to multiple severely
    constrained devices. The framework analyzes the network's devices and their
    capabilities, partitioning the model into appropriate sized segments and
    deploying them to the devices, according to their capabilities. 
    The framework allows for inference tasks to be
    executed in a highly scalable manner, even in an environment of highly
    constrained devices, although it is limited in the model's topology
    flexibility, as it only supports chain-like \ac{DNN} models. 

    \item[\textcite{p_dai_joint_2025}] 
    present a solution to the collaborative inference problem that optimizes
    device placement and model partitioning, accounting for particularities of 
    heterogeneous edge resources and \ac{CNN} models, with the objective to
    maximize throughput.
    A combination of intra-layer and interlayer partitioning techniques are 
    applied in the proposed approach, including layer-wise partitioning for 
    interlayer partitioning, and channel and spatial segmentations
    for intra-layer partitioning.
    This approach reformulates the network as 
    a pipeline system, where each device is responsible for simultaneous 
    inference batches. The \ac{DNN} model segments are tailored for the devices 
    in the network and assigned according to the network topology, that can be 
    represented as a \ac{DAG}. The devices in the network can have sequential 
    or parallel relationships, that affect the assigned model segment.

    \item[\textcite{w_miao_adaptive_2020}]
    explore an approach based on splitting and deploying a given \ac{DNN} model 
    with a \ac{DAG} topology to several devices, exploiting parallelizable 
    segments (branches), based on model data and computation time estimations 
    for each layer and branch processing.
    The characteristics of this network are also taken into account, such as, 
    its bandwidth and the number of devices present in the network. 
    The authors also allow the network to be based on devices with homogeneous 
    computation times or heterogeneous, given the computation time estimations
    of each layer and branch for each device.
    This work also points out the unreliability and security concerns in 
    partitioning the model between data centers and edge devices.

\end{description}

Unlike traditional cloud-based inference systems, the reviewed frameworks
leverage the computational capabilities of edge and far-edge devices to
distribute the inference workload. This distribution not only reduces latency
but also optimizes resource utilization across the network, allowing for a 
faster and more power efficient processing.

Several techniques can be employed to achieve this effect, such as model partitioning,
which can be done in different ways: either by bi-partitioning the model between
far-edge and edge devices, or by locally partitioning the model among a collection
of devices closer to the far-edge layer. On the other hand, some frameworks employ
partitioning on a per-layer basis, while others rely on graph-based partitioning
techniques to better adapt to the model topology.

Another important architecture used to achieve a more efficient inference process
is Hierarchical Inference, which is accomplished by deploying 
differently sized \ac{ML} models through several hierarchies of devices, allowing
for a quick response in simple inputs where a smaller model is sufficient.

Characteristics of the latter strategy can be found in the partitioning based 
approaches, materialized in the early-exit mechanisms. Early-exits allow the load to 
be distributed, reaching only the 
more powerful devices when the inference task is more complex, while still reducing
the overall latency, energy consumption and bandwidth usage.

\section{Network Node Management} \label{sec:se32}

In a distributed system, the orchestration and management of every node of a
given network is of utmost importance. Since we are reviewing collaborative
frameworks, we will be analyzing how the presented frameworks strategize the 
scheduling and communications between their devices. Furthermore, the frameworks 
will be separated into two classes, heuristical and reinforcement learning 
scheduling approaches.

\subsection{Heuristical Approach} \label{sec:se321}

Predicting the assignments that lead to the most efficient processing of any
given inference task has been revealed to be a highly complex problem. Therefore,
several works have relied on heuristical approaches to simplify the problem
avoiding the loss of a big part of the desired efficiency. In this subsection
we will be reviewing the frameworks that rely on heuristics, which heuristics are
chosen and how the network is managed.

\begin{description}[style=unboxed, leftmargin=1cm, font=\bfseries]

    \item[CAS \cite{wang_context-aware_2021}]
    makes use of "the neighbor effect" and assigns the initial partitions 
    to the first devices, closer to the user, and allocates the tail layers to
    devices closer to the cloud, noted to be heavier on computation time
    even if lighter on the data. All the devices' constraints
    are gathered in a previous stage and taken into account. Network conditions
    are monitored in real-time, allowing the framework to strive for an optimal
    partitioning and deployment, adapting to network changes, such as device power 
    and network bandwidth fluctuations, as well as user requirement of latency. 

    \item [CoFANN \cite{tran-van_cofann_2025}]
    is a minimalist framework, in regard to network management, taking only
    into account a pair of devices. Nonetheless, it looks to minimize inference
    time and data transfers by searching thoroughly a continuous search space
    created from
    factors in the inference process. To minimize the data transfer, the initial
    raw data is processed in the end device and condensed as an intermediate 
    feature tensor to be wirelessly transmitted to an edge server.
    %TODO rever separação layout/flow vs network management

    \item[DINA \cite{t_mohammed_distributed_2020}]
    approaches the scheduling problem using matching games, based on game theory.
    Matching is obtained according to preference relations, similarly to 
    partitioning, which are derived by means of utility functions, representing 
    the benefit obtained by executing a given task on that device. The initial 
    matching is further improved by proposing swapping edge nodes between two 
    user nodes, if it benefits both parties.

    \item[NAMP \cite{rego::namp}]
    implements a heuristical scheduling algorithm to optimize the inference 
    process, taking into account the chosen programmable heuristics, such as 
    inference time or resource utilization. The framework gathers the devices' 
    characteristics and determines the optimal partitioning and deployment
    according to the chosen heuristics only before deployment, without further
    responsiveness to network changes. The deployment is achieved by using
    the Zephyr RTOS\footnote{Linux Foundation, Zephyr Project, \url{https://www.zephyrproject.org/}}
    with the CoAP protocol, to further minimize the overhead 
    of the communication between devices.


    \item[\textcite{p_dai_joint_2025}]
    use device placement to assign the model partitions to the devices.
    The feature extractor segment of the \ac{CNN} models is distributed along the
    network, while the classifier, as it requires less computation, is assigned to
    a singular device, given it has sufficient available memory for the corresponding
    weights. Intermediate inference results are exchanged between devices
    through a wireless link, allowing for a higher throughput.
    This solution assumes that the pipeline supports asynchronous communication.

    \item[\textcite{w_miao_adaptive_2020}]
    minimize the inference time relying on estimations of the computing time
    needed at each step. Since the inference time is determined by the
    edge device that gets results last, they approach scheduling by reducing 
    it to a load balancing problem. The data offloading between devices is 
    implemented by \ac{RPC}s.

\end{description}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Approach} \label{sec:se322}

Rather than relying on simple heuristics to achieve a near-optimal
solution, the works in this subsection build reinforcement learning models to 
capture the complex relationship between the network and the optimal assignment
to a greater extent. 

\begin{description}[style=unboxed, leftmargin=1cm, font=\bfseries]

    \item[\textcite{chen_deep_2024}]
    employ a \ac{DRL}-based algorithm to determine the optimal partitioning point,
    between end (our "Far-Edge") and edge devices. The work elaborates a special 
    algorithm that
    branches from the traditional Deep Q-Network (DQN), to improve decision-making
    efficiency, by storing and reusing past experiences, as well as by having a dual 
    estimation stream, one for state-value function and one for action-value function. 
    Nevertheless, the framework also regards the user's priorities, integrated in
    experience memory sampling. This allows the framework to minimize the inference
    latency or the energy consumption, according to the user's needs. 
    The network also makes use of ZeroMQ\footnote{\url{https://zeromq.org/}} 
    for communication between devices, using a PUSH-PULL pattern for data transfer.  

    \item[\textcite{ma_joint_2025}]
    make use of a \ac{DRL}-based algorithm, employing an actor-critic model to
    determine the optimal resource assignment and the layer of the inference task 
    to mark the bi-partitioning point. The \ac{PPO} algorithm is used to train the 
    model, optimizing cost evolution, balancing energy consumption and inference 
    latency. This approach takes into account all relevant system information, 
    including task characteristics, device capabilities, and network conditions.
    These conditions include the layer's required \acp{FLOP}, computational 
    capabilities, power consumption at each device, network's channel bandwidth, 
    among other relevant factors. 
    This information is used to make informed decisions on resource allocation
    and task partitioning.

\end{description}

To manage efficiently a network of devices, it is crucial to have a robust
strategy for scheduling and communication. The reviewed frameworks employ
different approaches to tackle this problem, ranging from simpler heuristics
to more complex reinforcement learning algorithms. 

On one hand, heuristical approaches provide a straightforward and computationally
light solution to the scheduling problem, while also being deterministic. They 
often rely on predefined rules or optimization techniques to allocate 
tasks and manage communication between devices, avoiding the need for more complex
computation as required by the reinforcement learning approaches (i.e., training).
These methods thrive on scenarios where the network conditions are relatively 
stable and predictable. However, they may struggle to adapt to dynamic 
environments or complex network topologies, lacking the overall flexibility and 
robustness.

The most common approaches to these heuristic scheduling algorithms are based on
estimating the computational load and communication overhead for each device and 
subtask, and then using a search algorithm to find a near-optimal assignment of 
tasks to devices. This method, if not adapted, may lead to suboptimal performance,
as the estimations end up overlooking any particularities of the devices and the
network, including fluctuations in performance, transmission and other factors that
may further hinder the system performance. 

On the other hand, reinforcement learning approaches offer a more sophisticated
solution by allowing the system to learn optimal scheduling policies through
interaction with the environment. These methods can adapt to changing network
conditions and can potentially discover more efficient scheduling strategies.
However, they often require more computational resources and time to train the
learning models, as well as enough data from the network to make informed decisions. 

The communication between devices is another critical aspect of network management.
Efficient communication protocols and methods can significantly impact the overall
performance of the collaborative inference process. The reviewed frameworks
utilize various communication methods, even if not always explicitly specified (i.e.,
the wireless links referred to in Table~\ref{tab:sota_scheduling}). 
These methods include ZeroMQ, CoAP protocol, and \ac{RPC}s, each with the goal
of minimizing communication overhead and latency while ensuring reliable data
exchange.

The previously described frameworks that were not mentioned in either of these
network management categories do not present any particular strategy for network 
management, relying on previous works or simple approaches.

\section{Final Remarks}

%TODO rever conclusões gerais do SOTA - referir o que vou usar?
In this chapter, we have analyzed the current state of the art in collaborative
inference frameworks for edge and far-edge devices. We have reviewed various
approaches and techniques employed to optimize the inference process, focusing
on system layouts and network management strategies.

Each framework presents unique solutions to the challenges posed by distributed
inference tasks. Some works focus on model partitioning strategies to maximize
the use of available resources, while others emphasize efficient scheduling and
communication methods to minimize latency and overhead.

We have reviewed frameworks according to their system layouts. Some frameworks rely
on Hierarchical Inference, deploying differently sized \ac{ML} models across several
sets of devices according to their computational capabilities. Others rely on model
partitioning techniques to distribute the inference workload across the network, such
as the Model Chain Partitioning and Model Local Partitioning approaches. The former
involves dividing the inference task into segments deployed to different network
layers with different computational capabilities. The latter deploys the entire
partitioned model locally to a cluster of devices closer to the
far-edge layer.

Furthermore, we have analyzed the network management strategies employed by
the reviewed frameworks. We categorized them into heuristical and reinforcement
learning approaches, providing a range of possible solutions from simple and fast
heuristics to more complex and adaptive reinforcement learning algorithms.

The diversity of approaches highlights the complexity of the problem and the
need for tailored solutions depending on the specific requirements and constraints
of the target environment. An overview of the reviewed strategies is presented
in Table~\ref{tab:sota_overview}, summarizing their key features and relevant
aspects.

    Employs a bi-hierarchical fog computing architecture to execute inference tasks.
    Deploys partitioned \ac{DNN} models with \ac{DAG} topologies to homogeneous
    devices. Adapts to reduce execution time in varying network conditions.
&  
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Extension of a local inference task to a distributed edge device network.
        \item Partition and compression techniques for \ac{CNN} models.
        \item Matching theory for scheduling purposes.
    \end{itemize}
\\

\textcite{e_m_k_adaptive_2025}
&
    Hierarchical Inference system with three layers of models, from \acp{MCU}
    to cloud-based models. Highly adaptable framework, although it uses a 
    simplistic offloading decision module.
&
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Coordination between three layers of models, from \acp{MCU} to cloud-based models.
    \end{itemize}
\\
\hline
\textcite{ma_joint_2025} 
& 
    Framework that minimizes energy consumption in inference networks by employing
    early-exit mechanisms and bi-partitioning of \ac{DNN} models. The framework
    relies on a \ac{DRL}-based algorithm to optimize resource assignment and
    partitioning point.
&  
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Use of early-exit mechanisms to reduce energy consumption and latency.
        \item \ac{DRL}-based scheduling approach using \ac{PPO} algorithm.
    \end{itemize}
\\
\hline
NAMP \cite{rego::namp}
&
    Framework that emphasizes resource utilization, partitioning the model into
    several segments, fit for multiple severely constrained devices. The framework 
    is flexible and makes the most use of the network's heterogenous resources,
    as well as allows for the user to choose the heuristics to optimize.
&
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Configurable heuristics to optimize.
        \item Use of Zephyr RTOS and CoAP protocol to minimize communication overhead.
    \end{itemize}
\\
\hline
\textcite{p_dai_joint_2025} 
& 
    Widely applicable framework for collaborative inference in edge networks,
    optimizing throughput and adapting partitioning according to each device's 
    capabilities. The framework has been shown to be a highly scalable,
    robust and efficient solution.
& 
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Partition and parallelization techniques for \ac{CNN} models.
        \item Device placement and network topology considerations for scheduling. 
    \end{itemize}
\\

\textcite{r_g_pacheco_calibration-aided_2021}
&
    Specialized work in early-exit mechanisms for split inference,
    even if limited to two-device networks. The framework excels in
    inserting early-exit branches in intermediate layers of \ac{DNN}
    models, while addressing the overconfidence problem.
&
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Effective early-exits insertion mechanism.
        \item Calibration techniques to address overconfidence.
    \end{itemize}
\\

& 
    Collaborative inference framework that takes advantage of parallelizable

    adapts to heterogeneous devices, balancing the load between them to
    minimize the overall inference time.
& 
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item Load balancing approach for heterogeneous devices.
