\chapter {Experiments and Results} \label{chap:ch5}

In this chapter, we present the core segment of this work. Firstly, the experimental
setup is described, detailing the network topology, the energy measurement process, 
and the models used for validation of our framework as well as to gather the data for
the analysis of the results. Then, the performance metrics of the different partitioning
strategies and distribution configurations are presented and analyzed, with a focus on
the trade-offs between latency and energy consumption, aiming to profile the
impacts of these strategies on the collected metrics and understand the potential
benefits of collaborative inference in edge and far-edge devices.

\section {Experimental Setup} \label{sec:se51}

In a effort to validate our framework and also to understand the impact of
partitioning and distributing the inference process across multiple devices, we
conducted a series of experiments. The experiments were designed to evaluate the
performance, in terms of latency and energy consumption, of different partitioning
strategies and distribution configurations across a set of models with varying
complexity and architecture.

\subsection {Network Topology} \label{sec:se511}

To put the framework into practice, we set up a local network consisting of four
Particle Argon\footnote{https://docs.particle.io/argon/} microcontrollers and a
single Raspberry Pi 3 Model B\footnote{https://www.raspberrypi.com/products/raspberry-pi-3-model-b/},
all connected via Wi-Fi. This network is controlled and monitored by an external
computer, which is responsible for orchestrating the experiments, collecting data, 
and processing results.

% - Why just one Raspberry Pi? - One to allow bigger models, no more so it doesnt gravitate into only rpi usage
The choice of using a single Raspberry Pi was motivated by the need to have a more
powerful device to handle the inference tasks of more complex models, expanding the
range of models our framework can support and evaluate. At the same time, we wanted
to maintain a balance and mitigate the bias toward a Raspberry Pi-centric execution
for all experiments. 

This network structure allows us to better understand the 
trade-offs and possible benefits of having \acp{MCU} in the network, 
and take advantage of their potential in other real-life heterogeneous networks, as
it keeps a balance between higher performant and power consuming devices, and lower
performant and power efficient devices.

For the \ac{MCU} devices, we
emulated the execution of each possible partition to determine the $Arena\ Size$,
the memory needed to allocate for all the intermediate tensors, from the input to the 
output, to execute the segment. Looking further into the used embServe service's 
template and \ac{MCU} image, we could establish a lower bound for the memory footprint
of each segment, as presented in Equation~\ref{eq:memory-footprint}. Not only, the
size of the buffers used to store the input and output tensors, but also the submodel 
as a whole.

\begin{equation}
  Memory\ Footprint = 2 \times Input\ Size + Output\ Size + Arena\ Size + Model\ Size
  \label{eq:memory-footprint} 
\end{equation}

To aid on the analysis of the memory footprint of each submodel, we created a table
for each model, like the one presented in Figure~\ref{fig:all-cnn-c-partitioning}.
In these tables, rows represent the starting layer of the submodel and
columns represent the ending layer of the submodel. Each cell in the table represents
the corresponding submodel, and is color-coded based on its memory footprint.
Yellow cells represent a memory footprint
above 55~kB, but below 80~kB, which is the maximum available memory for the Particle
Argon \ac{MCU}, after accounting for the memory used by the operating system
and embServe. Similarly, red cells represent a memory footprint above the 80~kB limit.


\subsection {Energy Measurement} \label{sec:se512}

In addition, we connected an Argon \ac{MCU} to a Joy-IT USB-Multimeter 120~\footnote{https://joy-it.net/en/products/JT-UM120},
a device that reports a variety of electrical parameters, including voltage, current,
and power consumption in real time. Sampling the circuit at a 10~ms interval, with
an accuracy of five decimal places.

We ran each submodel that participated in the
inferences included in the experiments, and collected the data from the multimeter, 
which allowed us to profile the energy consumption of each submodel, and, by extension, 
the energy consumption of the entire inference process. This process was repeated
for the Raspberry Pi, to collect data on the energy consumption of the submodels that
were deployed on it.  

To isolate the energy consumption of the inference process, we also measured the idle
consumption of the devices, that is, the energy consumed by the devices when they are
not performing any user task, and subtracted the corresponding energy value from the
total energy consumption. For the Argon \ac{MCU}, the idle consumption was measured to
be $0.135026~W$, while for the Raspberry Pi, the idle consumption was measured to be 
$1.271197~W$.

\section {Models} \label{sec:se52}

To validate our framework, we selected a set of models that are representative of the
edge computing domain, with varying levels of complexity and architecture. These models
try to cover a wide range of applications and use cases, from simple image classification
tasks to wake word and anomaly detection. All this while keeping a focus on
model size for the constraints of the devices on the edge and far-edge. The models we
selected, partitioned, distributed across the network, and had the performance evaluated
will be described in the following subsections. 

\subsection {All-CNN-C} \label{sec:se521}

The first model we present is the All-CNN-C model~\cite{all-cnn-c-springenberg2015},
an image classification model selected for its architectural simplicity and efficiency.
This decision was primarly motivated by the fact that it is a simple \ac{CNN} with
the peculiarity of being composed entirely of convolutional layers, replacing the
traditionally used pooling and fully connected layers with strided convolutions. 
This makes it a good candidate, as the layer types are similar to eachother, making 
the computational load uniform along the network, and, therefore, allowing for
a large amount of possible balanced partitions to distribute.

\subsubsection {Model Architecture} \label{sec:se5211}

The All-CNN-C architecture is characterized by its uniform application of 
convolutional layers. Unlike standard architectures that reduce spatial resolution 
via max-pooling, All-CNN-C utilizes strided convolutions to downsample the input. 

The network comprises nine convolutional layers, followed by a global average pooling 
layer. Specifically, the architecture applies
a series of $3 \times 3$ convolutions, with specific layers utilizing a stride of 2
to reduce spatial dimensions. The network concludes with a global average pooling 
layer that maps the resulting feature maps into a single vector, which is then 
passed to a softmax layer for classification, as represented in
Figure \ref{fig:all-cnn-c-architecture}. It should be noted that, to further compact
the model to fit the devices constraints, we used a width multiplier of $\alpha=0.125$,
that limit the number of filters in each convolution, and, therefore, the size of 
intermediate feature maps.

This design effectively eliminates the parameter-heavy fully connected layers, 
significantly reducing the model's memory footprint while maintaining competitive 
accuracy on benchmarks such as CIFAR-10.

\subsection {DS-CNN} \label{sec:se522}

The second architecture used to evaluate the performance of different heuristics is the 
\ac{DS-CNN} model~\cite{ds-cnn-zhang2018helloedge}, which is a highly optimized, 
lightweight convolutional neural network designed for efficient keyword spotting and
localized inference on resource-constrained devices, such as edge devices. 
This model shifts away from traditional computationally heavy 3D convolutions by 
uncoupling spatial and channel-wise feature extraction, 
which significantly reduce the number of parameters and computational cost in each
convolutional layer. This makes \ac{DS-CNN} an exemplary model for TinyML, as it can be 
flexibly partitioned and distributed across a given network of devices, without
having its evaluation performance compromised.


\subsubsection {Model Architecture} \label{sec:se5221}

The model configuration utilized in our evaluation corresponds to the \ac{DS-CNN} 
delineated in the core \textit{Hello Edge}~\cite{ds-cnn-zhang2018helloedge} 
framework, with a width multiplier of $\alpha=0.25$, to further help us fit the model 
to the constraints of far-edge devices.  

The network architecture, represented in Figure \ref{fig:ds-cnn-architecture}, 
initiates with a standard 2D convolutional layer, 
which acts as a primary feature processor over the input time-frequency spectrogram 
data. This baseline 
layer prepares the multi-channel feature representation 
by mapping local correlations across a predefined set of feature channels.

Following the initial standard convolution, the core feature extraction pipeline is 
composed of four sequential depthwise separable convolutional blocks. Each block 
factorizes the standard convolution mechanism into two isolated operations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Depthwise Convolution:} Applies a dedicated 2D spatial filter per 
    individual input channel independently to capture localized temporal and spectral 
    features.
    \item \textbf{Pointwise Convolution:} Implements a $1 \times 1$ convolution across 
    the full channel depth, linearly combining the separate outputs of the depthwise 
    phase and projecting them into a brand new channel space.
\end{itemize}

Every independent convolutional operation within these blocks is structurally bound to 
a subsequent Batch Normalization layer followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) 
activation function, ensuring robust optimization stability during the forward pass. 
The network is concluded by a global average pooling layer that compresses the dimensional 
spatial map into a singular feature vector, which is subsequently routed through a fully 
connected softmax layer to compute the localized classification probabilities.

\subsection {MobileNetV1} \label{sec:se523} 

The third and most complex model we present is the MobileNetV1\cite{mobilenet-howard2017},
a highly popular architecture in the mobile and edge computing domain, designed for
image classification and object detection, among other computer vision tasks, while
maintaining a low computational footprint. This model poses a significant challenge, by
its sheer size and complexity, but also presents itself as an opportunity to collect 
data closer to a real-life scenario, as it is a model that is widely used and 
integrated in a variety of applications and frameworks.

\subsubsection {Model Architecture} \label{sec:se5231}

MobileNetV1 is built upon the concept of depthwise separable convolutions, as 
previously described in section \ref{sec:se5221}. However, it introduces a more complex,
modular structure, utilizing 82 total layers.
Unlike the static blocks in \ac{DS-CNN}, MobileNetV1 employs a highly efficient 
architecture that facilitates hyperparameter tuning through width and resolution 
multipliers, allowing the model to be scaled according to specific latency or accuracy 
constraints. 

The architecture relies on several blocks, where a depthwise convolution  % bottleneck block?
is followed by a pointwise convolution, both bundled with Batch Normalization and ReLU6 
activations. This structure provides a significantly deeper feature extraction path, 
making it an ideal candidate for evaluating how our partitioning framework handles long, 
complex computational chains across heterogeneous edge devices.

In our evaluation, we utilize the MobileNetV1 with an width multiplier of $\alpha=0.25$, an
input resolution of $32 \times 32 \times 3$, and a final output layer of 256 classes.
Additionally, we also cropped the model to remove the last two blocks, as they
represented a challenge to the memory constraints of the far-edge devices, and would
have further limited the number of possible partitions to distribute across the network.

This approach, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:mobilenet-architecture}, was chosen to 
maintain a balance between model complexity and the constraints of our far-edge devices, 
while still providing a realistic scenario for tests.

\subsection {MLP Autoencoder} \label{sec:se524} 

The last model we present is a simple \ac{MLP} autoencoder, fit for anomaly detection.
This model was selected for its almost archetypal architecture, which allows us to 
evaluate the performance of the partitioning and distribution strategies in the most
simple scenario, that is often used in more complex models as a building block.

\subsubsection {Model Architecture} \label{sec:se5241}

The autoencoder model is a \ac{MLP} composed of a simple feedforward architecture, with 
an input layer, a set of hidden layers, and an output layer. 

The initial layers are designed to
process the data and extract relevant features, encoding them into a lower-dimensional,
latent space. The subsequent layers then decode this representation back into the
original input space, aiming to reconstruct the input data as accurately as possible,
as represented in Figure \ref{fig:autoencoder-architecture}. 

The model is trained to minimize the reconstruction error, and by training the model on
normal data, as it learns to reconstruct the normal patterns, it can be used to detect 
anomalies by identifying instances where the reconstruction error exceeds a certain
threshold.

In particular, this model is composed an encoder of four blocks, with 96 neurons each, 
a middle block composed of 8 neurons, a decoder of four blocks, similar to the encoder,
with 96 neurons each, and an output layer with 96 neurons. Each mentioned block is
composed of a dense layer, followed by a batch normalization layer and an activation
layer, with the exception of the output layer, which does not have an activation layer.

\section {Overall Results Analysis} \label{sec:se53}

Considering the results from all the models, we can take note of a few patterns that
emerge from using the different partitioning and distribution strategies.

Regarding latency, the Edge Only strategy consistently outperforms the other strategies,
followed by the Edge First, Edge Last, and Min-Cut strategies, which have similar
results between them. This pattern likely stems from the fact that these strategies
rely on fewer devices, and therefore, fewer communication steps, which is a significant
factor, as noticed in the active inference latency and overhead latency breakdown
graphs.

Another pattern that emerges, specially in the \ac{DS-CNN} model, as both the
Edge Only and Greedy strategies rely on a single device, is that even with only one
device, the Greedy strategy takes longer to complete the inference process, as the

In terms of energy consumption, the results are shown to scale, firstly, with the
number of devices that partake in the inference process, and secondly, with the
type of devices that are used. The edge device only networks, that is, the Edge Only
strategies, are the most energy efficient, followed by the mixed device networks, and
finally, the far-edge device only networks. %TODO with exception of DS-CNN Greedy?

Even if not represented in the graphs, the idle energy consumption of the devices is a
significant factor in the trade-off evaluation between the different strategies. The 10 
times idle energy consumption difference between the Argon \ac{MCU} and the Raspberry 
Pi can be a determining factor, as the overall network idle time increases or decreases.  

Nonetheless, each distribution strategy has its own merits and can be more suitable for
a specific use case, depending on the model architecture and network topology. The
Edge First or Edge Last strategies can be more suitable for networks with the
\acp{MCU} devices closer to sensors or actuators, correspondingly. While the Min-Cut
strategy reveals the significance of the intermediate tensor size and the Greedy and
Load-Balanced strategies enphasize the importance of decreasing the number of devices
used for the inference process, and therefore, the number of communication steps.

In the end, the results indicate that the inclusion of edge devices in the inference
process is only beneficial if the required throughput is above a certain threshold.
In other words, if the inference process is not time sensitive and time sparse, the 
inclusion of edge devices in the inference process is not beneficial.
